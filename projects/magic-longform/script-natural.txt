In nineteen thirty-six, a box of old papers went up for auction in London. They belonged to Isaac Newton, the man who explained gravity, light, and the motion of the planets.

Buyers expected equations. What they found... was magic.

Recipes for the Philosopher's Stone. Secret symbols. Instructions for turning lead into gold. Newton wrote around a million words on alchemy, more than he ever wrote about physics.

The smartest man of his age spent thirty years trying to do magic.

He failed. But... what if he hadn't?

What if magic was real? Not in some fantasy world, but here, on Earth, in our history, under the same laws of physics we live by. The answer is stranger than any fantasy book.

Because real magic would have a price, and you would pay it with your own body.

Let's set one rule. Magic can't create energy from nothing. Nothing can. So the only fuel a human carries... is food. An adult burns about two thousand calories a day.

That's roughly eight megajoules: the entire daily budget of a wizard.

So what does a spell actually cost? Levitating yourself ten metres into the air takes about seven kilojoules. Less than two calories. A single grape. Boiling a kettle with your bare hands?

About eighty calories. One banana.

And a fireball strong enough to throw a soldier across a battlefield? Roughly the energy of a stick of dynamite.

Here's the strange part: a chocolate bar holds about as much energy as that stick of dynamite. The dynamite just releases it all at once.

That's what magic really is. Not more power. Faster power. A well-fed mage could throw maybe five fireballs a day... and then collapse.

So the first great wizards wouldn't look like wise old men with long beards. They'd look starving. And they would eat... constantly.

Rewind a hundred thousand years, to a small band of hunters on a freezing night. One of them can spark fire without flint. Another can feel water under the ground.

A third can close a wound with her hands.

In a world where a single infection could kill you, that last person is the most valuable human alive.

If magic runs in families, those families survive more winters, raise more children... and slowly, over thousands of generations, magic spreads.

But food is scarce. A tribe has to choose: feed the hunters, or feed the healer. That choice becomes the first law. The first politics. The first priesthood.

Our word magic comes from the Magi, priests of ancient Persia who were said to read the stars. In this world, they don't just read the stars. They command storms.

Temples become the first laboratories, where spells are tested, recorded, and guarded. Kings don't build armies of spearmen. They build granaries.

Because the army with the most food has the most magic.

Siege warfare changes forever. Walls don't stop someone who can float over them. So cities change shape. Watchtowers that look up, not out. Archers trained to hit a flying man.

And the most guarded building in every kingdom... isn't the palace. It's the kitchen.

In our real history, Europe executed somewhere between forty and sixty thousand people for witchcraft, for magic that never existed.

One of the last was in seventeen eighty-two, in Switzerland. Now imagine the same fear... except some of the accused can actually do it.

Two futures split here. In one, mages rule: a new nobility, born with power instead of land. In the other, they're registered, licensed, and watched, like weapons that walk and talk.

Most likely? Both. Mage guilds strike deals with kings: our service, in exchange for our safety. And the guilds keep one secret above all others: how magic actually works.

Which brings us back to Newton. In our world, the economist John Maynard Keynes, who bought many of those papers, said Newton wasn't the first of the age of reason.

He was the last of the magicians.

In a world with real magic, Newton doesn't have to choose. He measures spells the way he measured light. How much energy does a spell take? How far does it reach?

Does it fade with distance, like gravity?

And the moment you can measure magic, it stops being a mystery. It becomes engineering. Spellbooks turn into textbooks. Wizards turn into... technicians.

But here's where the dream runs into a wall. Remember the budget: eight megajoules a day. About two and a half kilowatt-hours.

A typical American home uses about thirty kilowatt-hours every day. The most powerful wizard alive couldn't keep one house running.

And a single tank of petrol holds more energy than a mage burns in half a year. So magic would never replace the engine. Coal, oil, and electricity would still win on raw power.

Magic would win somewhere else: precision. A healer doesn't need much energy to fuse a broken bone. Just perfect control. A job that takes the body six weeks, done in a minute.

Engineers who seal a crack in a bridge by touch. Firefighters who pull the heat right out of a burning building. The future wouldn't be wizards instead of technology.

It would be wizards and technology.

But every power has a shadow. A pickpocket who can lift a wallet from across the street. A burglar who walks through a locked door.

So the world would build defences: magic detectors at airports, spell-proof vaults, police units trained to catch casters.

You'd need a licence to cast in public, like a driving licence, with a test, and points you can lose.

And the biggest question of all: fairness. If only one person in a hundred is born with magic... what do we owe everyone else? Would magic become the greatest inequality in history?

Or would we do what we always do... and build machines, so everyone could have it?

And that's the strange thing. Picture someone from Newton's time, standing in your room right now. You touch a wall, and light appears.

You speak into a glass rectangle, and someone on the other side of the planet answers. Carriages with no horses. Metal birds that carry hundreds of people across the ocean.

Medicine that stops plagues.

Arthur C. Clarke wrote: any sufficiently advanced technology is indistinguishable from magic.

Newton never found the Philosopher's Stone. But the science he started turned out to be the only magic that works for everyone. Maybe magic was never missing from our world.

Maybe we just built it, one discovery at a time.
