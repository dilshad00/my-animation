## [NOT SPOKEN] 1. Cold Open (est. 0:00-0:45)
> FICTION. Night globe with lightning flickering everywhere; it all stops. One white pillar on Mount Olympus. A figure walks down.
Every second of every day, lightning flashes somewhere on Earth about forty-five times.

Now imagine that one night, it stops.

Every storm on the planet goes dark at the same moment. The satellites built to watch for lightning record nothing. Ten seconds. A minute. An hour.

Then a single bolt comes down on the highest mountain in Greece. It doesn't fade. It stays, a pillar of white fire joining earth and sky, for nine minutes.

And when it finally goes out, someone is walking down the mountain.

For more than three thousand years, people have spoken his name. So what would really happen if Zeus came back? And why, after all this time, would he come back at all?

## [NOT SPOKEN] 2. The Myths: Rules of the Story (est. 0:45-2:35)
> MYTH. Tag "Greek myth". Scroll style. Cronus swallowing children, the stone, Crete, Titan war, Cyclopes forging the bolt, Metis, Lycaon's flood, the cottage.
First, a line we need to draw. What you're about to hear comes from ancient Greek mythology. These are stories, not history, and there is no evidence any of it happened. But they tell us exactly who we'd be dealing with.

The poet Hesiod, writing around seven hundred BC, describes a family at war with its own children. The Titan Cronus had been warned that a child would overthrow him, so he swallowed each baby as it was born. His wife hid the youngest on the island of Crete, and handed Cronus a stone wrapped in cloth. He swallowed that instead.

That hidden child was Zeus. He grew up, his brothers and sisters were vomited back into the world alive, and together they fought the Titans for ten full years. He freed the Cyclopes from beneath the earth, and in gratitude they gave him the weapon that decided the war: the thunderbolt.

Then the prophecy came for him. Zeus was warned that the goddess Metis would bear a son strong enough to replace him. So he did what his father had done. He swallowed her.

The king of the gods was afraid of one thing: his own replacement.

He also had rules. Homer's Odyssey says that all strangers and beggars come from Zeus. In a tale retold by the Roman poet Ovid, a king named Lycaon served the disguised god human flesh, and a flood drowned the world. But when a poor old couple, Baucis and Philemon, shared their last scraps with two dusty travellers, their cottage became a temple. The travellers were gods.

Remember that. It matters later.

Because this is where the ancient stories end, and ours begins. From here on, everything that happens in the modern world is invented.

## [NOT SPOKEN] 3. The Arrival (est. 2:35-3:33)
> FICTION. Tag "Hypothetical" from here to the end, except where a line is marked MYTH or REAL. Litochoro at dawn, phones dying, storm following him south, the fifteen columns in Athens.
He reaches the village of Litochoro at dawn. Witnesses film a barefoot man nearly three metres tall, and the air around him smells of burned rain. Every phone within a kilometre dies.

Within an hour, the footage from farther away has reached a billion screens. Within two, most of those screens have declared it fake.

He walks south for three days, and the storms travel with him, opening and closing over his head like a hand. Homer had a name for this. Cloud-gatherer.

In Athens, he goes to the one place he recognises. The Temple of Olympian Zeus took more than six centuries to complete. Today, fifteen of its columns still stand.

He puts his palm against one of them, and he stays there, silent, for an entire night.

At sunrise he speaks for the first time, in a Greek so old that only scholars can follow it. It's a question.

Who is left?

## [NOT SPOKEN] 4. The World He Finds (est. 3:33-4:43)
> REAL facts inside FICTION. Empty base at Olympia, small group of worshippers, satellites over Olympus, TV forecaster, lightning rod, power lines. MYTH beat: Prometheus on the rock.
The answer isn't kind. His great statue at Olympia, a seated giant of gold and ivory and one of the Seven Wonders of the Ancient World, is gone.

A few people do still honour the old gods. In two thousand seventeen, Greece recognised their faith as a known religion, and its followers are counted in the thousands. They come to the temple in tears. He looks at them for a long time.

Then he looks up.

Thousands of machines now circle above his clouds. On television, a cheerful woman tells farmers when it will rain, and nobody sacrifices anything.

But it's the lightning that stops him. Someone shows him a metal rod on a rooftop. Since the seventeen fifties, humans have been telling his thunderbolts where to land. The same force now runs through wires under every street. It charges our phones.

In the myths, the Titan Prometheus stole fire for humanity, and Zeus chained him to a rock, where an eagle ate his liver. So imagine his face when he learns what we did next.

We didn't stop at fire. We took the lightning too.

## [NOT SPOKEN] 5. Governments, Scientists, Everyone Else (est. 4:43-5:31)
> FICTION. Emergency council room, instruments around Zeus, lawyers, crowded churches and airports, someone going to work anyway.
Governments do what governments do. Emergency sessions. Closed airspace. Statements that say nothing, very carefully.

Scientists surround him with instruments, and the numbers make no sense. He gives off enormous energy with no visible source. After a month of work, an international panel publishes its conclusion, and it may be the most frightening sentence in the history of science.

We do not know what this is.

Everyone else splits apart. Lawyers begin arguing, in complete seriousness, whether an act of God clause covers a god who can be named. Houses of worship fill up across the planet.

And millions of people simply go to work, because the rent is still due.

Then a general asks the question nobody wants to say aloud.

What can he actually do?

## [NOT SPOKEN] 6. The Demonstration (est. 5:31-6:26)
> MYTH then FICTION. Titan war flashback, drones falling at a nod, bolt on an empty island, empty sea.
Hesiod describes the Titan war in terms that are hard to forget. When Zeus finally stopped holding back, the land seethed and the sea boiled.

Real lightning is already terrifying. A single channel can reach about thirty thousand kelvin, around five times hotter than the surface of the Sun. And that's only weather.

So when a nervous coalition sends a swarm of armed drones to contain him, he doesn't raise a hand.

He nods.

In Homer, one nod makes Olympus quake. Here, every drone within two hundred kilometres drops out of the sky.

Then, at noon, he turns toward a bare rock in the Aegean. The bolt is visible from Cairo. When the steam clears, the island is gone.

Nobody dies. And that is what terrifies the experts most. Of all the islands in the Aegean, he chose one with no one on it.

He knew.

## [NOT SPOKEN] 7. Faith and Power (est. 6:26-7:30)
> FICTION, respectful. Faith leaders calm and unnamed, no real faith shown as false. Leaders courting him. Reporter reads the names; storm stops.
For billions of believers, you might expect this to be the end of everything. It isn't. Theologians answer quickly and calmly. Enormous power is not the same thing as God. Zeus had parents. He seized a throne. He never claimed to have made the universe.

So most people go on praying exactly as they did before.

World leaders have the harder problem. Their authority rests on being the strongest force in the room, and now they aren't. Some send gifts. One offers an alliance. A few quietly ask for rain.

Then, at a press conference in Athens, a young reporter stands up and begins reading names from the old stories. Io. Europa. Callisto. Semele. Women whose encounters with Zeus ended in abduction, assault, transformation or death.

She asks if he's sorry.

In three thousand years of stories, nobody has put that to him. The storm above the city stops moving.

He doesn't answer. Because that same night, the sea goes flat, from Gibraltar to Beirut.

He isn't alone anymore.

## [NOT SPOKEN] 8. The Olympians Return (est. 7:30-8:35)
> FICTION with MYTH references. Poseidon at Piraeus, Athena on the scaffolded Parthenon, Hephaestus in a robotics lab, Hermes vanishing online, Ares walking toward smoke.
Poseidon, whom the Greeks called the Earth-Shaker, rises at the port of Piraeus with plastic tangled in his hair. Within the hour, every port on the Mediterranean is closed.

Athena climbs the Acropolis. Her temple, the Parthenon, was torn open in sixteen eighty-seven, when a shell hit the gunpowder stored inside it. She walks the scaffolding in silence. Then she asks to meet the people building thinking machines.

Hephaestus, god of the forge, once built golden servants that Homer says had minds and voices of their own. He walks into a robotics laboratory, and for the first time since the return, a god laughs.

Hermes, god of messengers and thieves, discovers the internet, and isn't seen for a week.

And Ares, god of war, is the only one who looks happy. He doesn't stop to speak with anyone. He just walks toward the smoke.

Which is strange, because in the Iliad, Zeus tells his own son, you are the most hateful to me of all the gods.

Now Zeus follows him.

## [NOT SPOKEN] 9. The Judgment (est. 8:35-9:47)
> MYTH then FICTION. Sarpedon and the bloody rain, Homer's named dead, drone screen, thirty thousand watchers vs cameras, nine warhead symbols, the reveal.
Zeus knows war. In the Iliad, he watches his mortal son Sarpedon walk toward death at Troy. He could save him. He lets it happen anyway, and rain falls on the battlefield, drops of blood, in honour of his child.

Homer gives the dead names. Hundreds of them.

Now Zeus stands behind a young soldier watching a screen, as a target half a world away turns to heat and silence.

Who was he, Zeus asks.

The soldier doesn't know.

Hesiod warned that Zeus had thirty thousand immortal watchers roaming the Earth, recording every injustice. Today, a single large city can have more cameras than that.

So the whole world asks him, at last. Why did you come back?

And he tells them.

He came for the prophecy. The child who would replace him. For all of time he had watched for it among the gods, and he had swallowed and schemed to stop it.

It was never a god.

Nine nations now hold weapons that could boil a sea. Fire. Lightning. Machines that think.

I feared a son, he says, and he looks out over the city. I should have feared you.

## [NOT SPOKEN] 10. Worship, Fear or Challenge (est. 9:47-10:47)
> FICTION with MYTH references. Three crowds, Typhon flashback, one flash over empty ocean, Zeus unharmed, one word, sky empty.
Humanity responds as it always has. All three ways at once.

Some worship him. Temples rise from old foundations, and strangers kneel in the streets.

Most fear him, and quietly start storing food.

And some decide to challenge him. They know the myths too. In the Iliad, a mortal named Diomedes wounds two gods in a single battle. Gods can bleed.

So one night, a rogue command fires a single warhead at the empty stretch of ocean where he stands alone.

The whole world watches the light fade.

He's still there.

Everyone knows what comes next. When the monster Typhon rose against him, with its hundred serpent heads, Zeus burned it and threw it into the pit of Tartarus.

Instead, Zeus smiles. Rising against the throne is exactly what he once did to his own father.

Good, he says.

By morning, every one of them is gone. And the sky goes quiet again. Not one flash, anywhere on Earth.

## [NOT SPOKEN] 11. The Knock (est. 10:47-12:05)
> FICTION, then the closing MYTH lines. Investigators' wall of footage; the same dusty old man on doorsteps worldwide; a woman sets out a second bowl; lightning returns; two jars; a door.
For months, nobody knows why. Then investigators, combing through footage from those weeks, notice something.

An old man. Dusty. Ordinary. At a bus station in Thessaloniki. Outside a shelter in Lagos. On a porch in Ohio. The same man, on thousands of doorsteps, at the very hour the giant stood in Athens.

He was knocking, and asking for something to eat.

The whole world had been watching the sky. He had been checking the doors.

Most stayed shut. But in a small flat, a woman with almost nothing warmed up what she had left, and set out a second bowl.

We never learn what he decided. Only that after the last door, the storms came back. Forty-five flashes every second, as if nothing had happened.

In the Iliad, Achilles says two jars stand on the floor of Zeus's palace, one of blessings and one of sorrows, and no one gets only the good. And when Pandora opened her jar, Hesiod says, one thing stayed behind. Hope.

None of this happened. Zeus is a story people have told for thousands of years, and it may be one of the oldest warnings we have. The next stranger could be anyone.

The thunder was never the test.

The knock was.
