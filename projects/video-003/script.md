## [NOT SPOKEN] 1. The Hook (est. 0:00-0:27)
> Night city seen from space. Lights go out region by region. Food on a table vanishes plate by plate. A hand holding a phone: phone dissolves, then the shoes, then the glasses. Ends on one hand holding a rock. Tag: REAL HISTORY for the fertiliser line.

Right now, about half of humanity is fed by one industrial process that most people have never heard of.

Take it away, and the food runs out for billions.

Now take away everything else. The engines. The medicine. The alphabet. Even the wheel.

Your house is gone. Your city is gone. And there's a very good chance you are too.

So what would be left, if technology had never moved past the Stone Age?

## [NOT SPOKEN] 2. What Technology Really Is (est. 0:27-1:16)
> Screens morph backwards: phone, radio, book, wheel, knot, sharpened stick. A timeline stretches off screen to 3.3 million years. Rules card: KEEP / NEVER INVENTED.

Here's the catch. When we say technology, we picture screens.

But a knot is technology. A basket is technology. A sharpened stick is technology.

And it's older than we are. In Kenya, at a site called Lomekwi, researchers found stone tools about three point three million years old. That's older than any known member of our own genus.

So a world with no technology at all doesn't have humans like us in it. We were shaped by our tools.

That means we need rules.

In this world, humans keep the early things: fire, stone, wood, bone, rope, boats carved from logs, and simple farming.

And then the clock stops. No metal. No wheel. No writing. No machines. Ever.

Everything old you're about to hear really happened. Whenever I say "in this world", we're guessing.

## [NOT SPOKEN] 3. Early Human Civilization (est. 1:16-1:58)
> Illustrated Stone Age camp at dusk, parallax hills, firelight on faces. Baby raised in a modern classroom cross-dissolve. Gobekli Tepe's carved pillars rising, tiny figures hauling stone. Tag: REAL HISTORY.

At first, almost nothing changes, because this is the world we really lived in for most of our history.

Small bands follow the animals.

And don't mistake them for simple. Biologically, they were us. A baby born ten thousand years ago and raised today would very likely learn to use your phone.

About eleven thousand years ago, at Gobekli Tepe in modern Turkey, people carved stone pillars weighing several tons and stood them in great circles. No metal. No wheels. And many of the builders were probably still hunting and gathering for much of their food.

But stone can only take you so far. To go further, you need something to eat.

## [NOT SPOKEN] 4. Food and Survival (est. 1:58-2:50)
> Fertile Crescent map, then a farmer on her knees with a digging stick. Skeleton-height comparison graphic. Population counter. Split: empty granary vs. a modern supermarket fading out. Tags switch REAL HISTORY to WHAT IF.

When farming began, more than ten thousand years ago, it arose independently in several parts of the world.

It's usually told as a triumph. It didn't feel like one.

Early farmers were often shorter and less healthy than the foragers before them.

But farming did one thing nothing else could. It fed more people.

Before farming, the entire human race may have numbered just a few million. By five thousand years ago, estimates range from about fourteen million to forty-five million.

In this world, that's where the climb begins to stall.

No iron plough to break heavy soil. No wheeled cart to bring the harvest in. No factory fertiliser to feed tired land. A digging stick, a stone sickle, and your back.

One bad season, and there's no truck coming with help.

And yet, hungry or not, people still choose to crowd together.

## [NOT SPOKEN] 5. Cities Without Engineering (est. 2:50-3:37)
> Catalhoyuk rooftops at sunrise: people walking across the roofs, ladders dropping into houses. Camera pushes down a hole in the roof. Then a WHAT IF city of mud spreads, smoke, rain dissolving walls.

Here's a surprise. You can build a town with almost none of this.

More than nine thousand years ago, thousands of people lived at Catalhoyuk, also in Turkey. It had no streets. The houses were packed wall against wall, so people walked across the rooftops and climbed down into their homes by ladder.

It worked. For around two thousand years.

But in this world, that may be close to the limit. Without bricks fired by the thousands, without metal tools, pipes or pumps, a big settlement fights the same three enemies forever: water, waste and fire.

Skyscrapers don't exist. Neither do sewers. A town's size is set by how far people can walk with food on their backs.

And that leads to a problem that shapes everything else.

## [NOT SPOKEN] 6. Transportation (est. 3:37-4:23)
> A route line across the United States: plane crosses in seconds, a walking figure barely moves as a calendar flips through months. Then an Andes road, a runner passing a knotted cord to the next runner. Tag: REAL HISTORY for the Inca.

The fastest way to cross a continent is your own two feet.

Today you can fly from New York to Los Angeles in about six hours. Walk it, at thirty kilometres a day, and it takes around five months, if you survive the trip.

Most people in this world might never travel more than a few days from where they were born.

But distance isn't unbeatable. The Inca ruled millions of people across the Andes with no horses and no wheeled vehicles. Their secret was relay runners called chasqui, who raced in short stages along mountain roads. Together, they could carry a message around one hundred and fifty miles in a day.

And that message wasn't always spoken. Sometimes, it was tied in string.

## [NOT SPOKEN] 7. Communication (est. 4:23-5:19)
> Khipu cords swing in the light. Elder and child by the fire; the same story passes through faces, generation after generation, as an ocean creeps over a coastline. Clay tablet: tally marks of barley. A genius in a hut, whose idea fades as the fire dies.

The Inca kept records on knotted cords called khipu. A knot could hold a number.

Now remove even that, and all you have is memory.

Don't underestimate it. Researchers have compared Aboriginal Australian stories about coastlines swallowed by the sea with the record of rising water. They argue that some of those stories may have been passed down for more than seven thousand years. Other scholars doubt it.

Still, memory has one terrible weakness.

The first writing we know of, from the city of Uruk over five thousand years ago, wasn't poetry. It was mostly bookkeeping. Barley in, barley out.

Yet that dull invention did something incredible. It let a dead person teach a stranger.

In this world, a genius works out how to stop a disease. She tells her village. And when that village is gone, the idea is gone too.

Which is a very bad thing, when you get sick.

## [NOT SPOKEN] 8. Medicine (est. 5:19-6:06)
> Peruvian skull with healed hole, bone regrowth animating. Healer with herbs at a bedside by firelight. A row of eight children's silhouettes; four fade. Smallpox particle over a world map, a counter climbing. A tiny scratch on an arm.

Ancient healers weren't helpless. In Peru, surgeons cut holes in living people's skulls, and by Inca times, healed bone shows that most of those patients survived.

But what they couldn't see were germs.

Across most of history, as far as researchers can tell, roughly half of all children died before they grew up. Half.

Look at your family, or your classroom, and imagine every second person gone before adulthood.

In the twentieth century alone, smallpox killed an estimated three hundred million people. Then vaccination helped wipe it from the planet.

In this world, there's no microscope and no vaccine. No antibiotics. No clean operating room. A cut from a thorn could kill you.

Death is close, and everybody knows it. So, naturally, people find reasons to bring it even closer.

## [NOT SPOKEN] 9. War and Government (est. 6:06-7:00)
> Lake Turkana landscape, skeletons in sand rendered tastefully as abstract outlines. Two stone-age bands facing off, spear points. Chief and elders in a circle; a map of tiny territories, none growing. Tag switches REAL HISTORY to WHAT IF.

Remove the guns, and you do not remove war.

At Nataruk in Kenya, around ten thousand years ago, a group of people appear to have died violently beside a lagoon, some with injuries from blows and projectiles. Not all researchers accept that it was a massacre. But few doubt that humans could kill each other long before metal.

In this world, the wars are smaller, and there are no nuclear weapons.

But small doesn't mean rare. Villages fight over water, food and revenge.

And government? Without writing, there are no long legal codes, no tax records, no paperwork to hold a distant province together. Power probably stays with chiefs, elders and whoever people can actually see.

Maybe a brilliant ruler builds something bigger, with knotted cords and runners. But without metal or wheels, it may not hold.

So fast-forward. What does this planet look like tonight?

## [NOT SPOKEN] 10. The World Today (est. 7:00-7:53)
> The opening space shot returns, now fully dark. Starlit sky, huge forests, rivers. Population number falling from 8 billion. A child looks up at the Milky Way. All tagged WHAT IF.

Look down from space, and the night side of Earth is almost completely dark.

There are eight billion of us in the real world. In this one, nobody can say for sure, but it could be closer to tens of millions.

The forests are vast. The rivers run clean. The climate isn't being warmed by burning coal and oil. Over a child's head, the Milky Way blazes across the whole sky.

It sounds like paradise.

But that child has a coin-flip's chance of growing up. Her mother could die giving birth to her. A single failed harvest could empty her village. And if an unknown illness arrives, nobody will ever learn what it was.

Almost certainly, nobody on this planet knows that Earth orbits the Sun. Nobody knows what stars are.

They have the same brains we do. They're just starting over, every single generation.

## [NOT SPOKEN] 11. The Conclusion (est. 7:53-8:33)
> Hand holding a rock, from the opening. Rock passes from hand to hand, through time: axe, wheel, pen, phone, ending in the viewer's hand. Final card: which one would you keep?

So what is technology, really?

It isn't your phone. It's a promise between strangers. Someone learned something, and made sure that you wouldn't have to learn it again.

That chain goes back more than three million years, to someone who picked up a stone and hit it just right.

It has given us cures and it has given us bombs. It can't tell us which ones to build. That part has always been up to us.

So look at whatever you're holding right now, and ask yourself: if everything vanished, and you could save just one invention for the people who come after you, which one would it be?
